Quantum machine learning for catheter supply chains:
elliptic-integral seasonality and harmonic modular-
form features
A finite multi-echelon framework for demand forecasting, safety stock and replenishment of sterile,
expiry-constrained catheter families - with an explicit test of whether the quantum layer adds anything

Independent research preprint
Version 1.0 | 18 November 2026 | Synthetic data only | No patient, customer or company-confidential data


  Abstract. Catheter supply chains must hold enough sterile, single-use, expiry-dated stock to protect procedure
  schedules, without holding so much that product expires on the shelf. That tension is driven by demand that is
  seasonal at several scales at once - annual procedure cycles, quarterly planning cycles and weekly scheduling
  cycles. We present a finite, auditable framework that (i) models a multi-echelon catheter network as a finite-
  state replenishment problem, (ii) represents non-sinusoidal seasonality exactly with complete elliptic integrals
  and Jacobi elliptic functions, (iii) builds a multi-scale feature dictionary from the Fourier coefficients of a
  harmonic Maass form and its shadow, and (iv) feeds those features to both a classical forecaster and a quantum-
  kernel machine, with replenishment routing stated as a Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
  (QAOA) problem. In a deterministic synthetic experiment (seed 7; four generic catheter families; 156 weeks),
  elliptic-modular features reduced test mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) from 17.25% for a trailing-mean
  baseline to 12.27% classically and 12.22% for a quantum-kernel emulation - a difference of 0.05 percentage
  points, well within noise. Fill rate rose from 97.25% to 98.71% (classical) and 98.75% (quantum-kernel
  emulation) at essentially unchanged average inventory (1,124 versus 1,133 units). The gain therefore comes
  from the features, not from quantumness. No quantum advantage is claimed, and no result here is a Boston
  Scientific forecast, plan or operating figure.


Keywords: supply chain management; catheters; quantum machine learning; quantum kernel; QAOA; elliptic integrals;
harmonic Maass forms; modular forms; inventory control; demand forecasting

1 A catheter supply chain fails in two directions at once
A missing catheter can delay or cancel a procedure. A surplus catheter is not harmless either: sterile barrier systems
have finite shelf life, storage is controlled, and expired product must be quarantined and destroyed. Unlike general
merchandise, catheter demand is also lumpy by design. A hospital does not consume ablation, balloon, diagnostic
and guiding catheters interchangeably; each family, and often each size within a family, is its own stock-keeping unit
(SKU) with its own demand process, lead time and clinical criticality.
Three properties make the forecasting problem distinctive. First, multi-scale seasonality: elective procedure
volumes follow an annual cycle, modulated by quarterly service planning and weekly operating-list patterns. A single
sinusoid fits none of these well. Second, asymmetric cost: the clinical and reputational cost of a stockout far exceeds
the holding cost of one more unit, so forecasts must be judged jointly with the inventory policy they feed, not by
error alone. Third, finite, regulated state: lot, expiry date, sterilisation status and unique device identification
constrain what can be shipped, so a useful model must be finite and auditable rather than an unconstrained black box.
This preprint makes four contributions. Section 2 states the finite replenishment model. Section 3 gives seasonality
an exact period through elliptic integrals. Section 4 derives a feature dictionary from harmonic modular forms.
Section 5 defines the quantum layer precisely enough to be falsified - and Section 6 falsifies any strong version of it
on synthetic data: the quantum-kernel emulation does not meaningfully beat the classical model using the same
features.

2 The finite multi-echelon replenishment model
Let the network be a finite directed graph G = (V, A). Nodes V comprise plants, regional distribution centres and
hospital-facing depots; arcs A carry lead times la in whole weeks. Let I be the finite set of catheter SKUs, partitioned
into families, and let T = {0, 1, ..., Tmax} be a finite weekly horizon. For SKU i at node v in week t, write on-hand
inventory xi,v,t  0, pipeline inventory pi,v,t, demand di,v,t and order quantity ui,v,t  0. Inventory balance, with expiry
loss ei,v,t  0 removed before demand is served, is

                       xi,v,t+1 = xi,v,t + arrivalsi,v,t  min(xi,v,t + arrivalsi,v,t, di,v,t)  ei,v,t                (1)

Unmet demand is treated as lost to the node in the week it arises - the conservative assumption for a procedure that
cannot wait - and is counted as a stockout si,v,t = max(0, di,v,t  xi,v,t  arrivalsi,v,t). The planning problem is the
finite-horizon stochastic programme

                            minu E [ t i,v ( hi xi,v,t + ci ui,v,t + i si,v,t + wi ei,v,t ) ]                       (2)

where hi is holding cost, ci unit replenishment cost, i the stockout penalty with i  hi for clinically critical
families, and wi the expiry write-off cost. Because every set is finite and every variable is bounded by storage and
shelf-life constraints, the state space is finite: the dynamic programme has a well-defined optimum, and any heuristic
- classical or quantum - can be scored against sampled evaluations of equation (2) rather than asserted to be better.

2.1 The (s, S) policy and safety stock
We operate the network under a weekly-review order-up-to policy. For SKU i with forecast weekly mean i,t,
forecast standard deviation i, lead time Li weeks and target cycle service level , the order-up-to level is


                                       Si,t = Li i,t + z i Li, z = 1()                                       (3)


with z0.95 = 1.645. Equation (3) is where forecasting meets operations: a forecaster that captures seasonality lowers
i around a time-varying mean, so the same service level can be held with a smaller safety-stock multiplier, or a
higher service level held at the same stock. Section 6 tests exactly that trade, using multipliers of 1.65 for the
baseline policy and 1.28-1.30 for the seasonal policies. Expiry enters through a first-expiry-first-out issuing rule and
the ei,v,t term in equation (2); the simulation in Appendix A counts stockouts and holding explicitly and treats expiry
qualitatively, which we flag as a limitation in Section 7.

2.2 What is stylised, and what is not claimed
The network topology is stylised: a plant tier, a regional distribution tier and a depot tier, with a three-week
replenishment lead time in the experiment. That structure is generic to medical-device distribution and is not a map
of any company's actual network, routing or lead times. The four product families - balloon, ablation, diagnostic and
guiding catheters - are generic catheter types, not named commercial products, and their demand levels in Section 6
are invented for the simulation. Nothing in this section should be read as a description of Boston Scientific's
operations.

3 Elliptic integrals give seasonal cycles an exact period
Seasonal demand is rarely sinusoidal. Procedure volumes rise, plateau while operating capacity binds, then fall - a
flattened, non-sinusoidal wave. Jacobi elliptic functions are the natural basis for such waves: they interpolate
between a sinusoid and a square wave through a single parameter, the elliptic modulus k  [0, 1). Their periods are
complete elliptic integrals, which is what makes the representation exact rather than fitted.

3.1 Complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds

                         K(k) = 0/2 (1  k2 sin2)1/2 d = 01 [(1  t2)(1  k2t2)]1/2 dt                                  (4)



                                            E(k) = 0/2 (1  k2 sin2)1/2 d                                                  (5)


K(k) is the quarter-period integral and E(k) the corresponding arc-length integral. Both admit the hypergeometric
closed form

                  K(k) = (  / 2 ) 2F1( 1/2, 1/2; 1; k2 ), E(k) = (  / 2 ) 2F1( 1/2, 1/2; 1; k2 )                            (6)


and K(k) diverges logarithmically as k  1, K(k) ~ ln( 4 / (1  k2) ). Table 1 evaluates equations (4)-(5) exactly as
computed by the code in Appendix A. The ratio K(k) / (/2) is the factor by which a non-sinusoidal seasonal cycle is
stretched relative to a sinusoid of the same amplitude - the quantity a planner actually needs when converting a fitted
wave into calendar time.

3.2 Jacobi elliptic seasonality
The Jacobi elliptic sine sn(u | k) has real period 4K(k), reduces to sin(u) at k = 0 and approaches a square wave as k
 1. We therefore write the seasonal factor for SKU i as

                     gi(t) = 1 + ai sn( 2K(ki) t / Pi | ki ) + bi sin(2t / 13) + ci sin(2t / 4.33)                           (7)

where Pi is the nominal annual period in weeks, and the two sine terms carry quarterly and roughly monthly
structure. The incomplete elliptic integral F( | k) = 0 (1  k2 sin2)1/2 d inverts sn, giving a phase variable in
which unevenly spaced observations - holiday-shortened weeks, for example - can be placed on a uniform seasonal
clock. In the synthetic experiment the generating process uses the equivalent multi-sine form and the fitted forecaster
recovers a week-of-year profile; equation (7) is the analytic form that profile approximates, and k = 0.70 is used as
the working modulus throughout.

4 Harmonic modular forms supply a multi-scale feature dictionary
Elliptic functions handle one seasonal scale exactly. A supply chain has several nested scales and needs a feature
family closed under rescaling. Modular forms provide one - modularity is a symmetry under rescaling of the upper
half-plane - and harmonic Maass forms extend it to the non-holomorphic functions that noisy seasonal data require.

4.1 Harmonic Maass forms and the shadow map
Let H = {  = x + iy : y > 0 } be the upper half-plane and q = e2i. A harmonic Maass form of weight k for SL2(Z) is
a smooth function f : H  C that transforms like a modular form of weight k, is annihilated by the weight-k
hyperbolic Laplacian

                          k = y2( 2/x2 + 2/y2 ) + iky( /x + i /y ), k f = 0                             (8)


and has at most linear exponential growth at the cusp. Every such form splits as f = f+ + f into a holomorphic part -
the mock modular form - and a non-holomorphic completion, with Fourier expansion

                           f() = n >>  c+(n) qn + n < 0 c(n) (1  k, 4|n|y) qn                             (9)


where (s, y) is the incomplete gamma function. The shadow operator

                                                k f = 2i yk f /                                              (10)


maps a harmonic Maass form of weight k to a cusp form of weight 2  k, its shadow. The pair (f+, kf) is the useful
object for applications: the holomorphic coefficients carry the sharp, periodic content, and the shadow carries the
smooth, decaying correction - the seasonal signal and the trend-and-disruption residual as two faces of one function.
This structure is classical: harmonic weak Maass forms were introduced by Bruinier and Funke, and Alfes, Griffin,
Ono and Rolen showed that Weierstrass mock modular forms encode central L-values of elliptic curves through
canonical weight-1/2 harmonic Maass forms - evidence that these coefficients carry genuinely structured, not
arbitrary, information.

4.2 From coefficients to supply-chain features
Set the modular parameter from the seasonal clock, t = t / P + i y0 with fixed height y0 > 0. The feature vector for
week t is the finite coefficient window

        (t) = ( c+(n) cos(2nt/P), c+(n) sin(2nt/P) )n=1..N  ( elliptic features from equation (7) )           (11)


Modularity, f( (a+b)/(c+d) ) = (c+d)k f(), becomes a consistency condition across time scales: the annual feature
set, rescaled to a quarter, must agree with the quarterly feature set up to the automorphy factor. In practice we do not
need a named Maass form. Any coefficient sequence satisfying a linear recurrence with the same multi-scale
consistency can serve, and in the experiment of Section 6 the dictionary reduces to the week-of-year profile it
induces. The modular-form language contributes a symmetry constraint and a coefficient dictionary, and its value
must be measured - as in Section 6 - against a baseline that lacks the constraint.

4.3 Periods, cycle integrals and a second elliptic link
The connection back to Section 3 is not decorative. Coefficients of half-integral-weight harmonic Maass forms are
given by period and cycle integrals of algebraic differentials on modular and elliptic curves, as Bruinier established.
A period of an elliptic curve E is itself an elliptic integral,  =  dx / (x3 + ax + b). The same integral therefore
prices the seasonal period in Section 3 and generates the feature coefficients here - one mathematical object doing
both jobs, which is the conceptual core of this preprint.

5 The quantum machine learning layer, stated falsifiably

5.1 Quantum feature map and kernel
Encode the classical feature vector (t) of equation (11) into an n-qubit state by a unitary feature map U, |(x) =
U(x) |0n. The quantum kernel is the squared overlap


                         Q(x, x) = |  (x) | (x)  |2 = | 0n| U(x) U(x) |0n |2                        (12)


A quantum support vector machine or kernel-ridge forecaster then solves the same dual problem as its classical
counterpart, with Q in place of a classical kernel (x, x) = exp(  ||x  x||2 ). The falsifiable claim is narrow: on
identical features, does Q forecast better than ? Section 6 answers it on synthetic data using a classical emulation
of the kernel values, because current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware - tens to a few hundred
noisy qubits, with state-preparation and data-loading costs that scale with the feature dimension - cannot yet run the
full pipeline at production scale. An emulation tests the kernel's mathematics; it does not test hardware speed, and we
claim nothing about hardware speed.

5.2 Replenishment and routing as QAOA
Binary replenishment decisions - replenish lane a in week t or not, za,t  {0, 1} - with lane costs and capacity
constraints form a quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation problem with cost Hamiltonian HC = i hi Zi + i<j Jij
ZiZj. The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm prepares


                   |(, ) = eipHB eipHC  ei1HB ei1HC |+m, min, | HC |                        (13)


with mixer HB = i Xi and depth p. We formulate the problem in this form so that a future fault-tolerant or improved
NISQ implementation can be benchmarked against the exact finite optimum of equation (2) on small instances. We
do not run QAOA in the experiment, and we make no claim that it outperforms classical solvers, which remain
extremely strong on problems of this size.

5.3 A sample-complexity bound, with its limits
For a kernel classifier with margin  trained on m samples, standard generalisation arguments give, with probability
at least 1  , a test error bounded by the empirical margin error plus a term of order ( log(1/) / (m 2) ). The
bound applies to the quantum kernel exactly as it does to a classical one, because it depends on the kernel only
through the margin it achieves. That is the theoretical counterpart of the empirical result in Section 6: nothing in the
standard theory promises a quantum-kernel advantage on structured, low-dimensional seasonal features, and none is
found.

6 Synthetic experiment: the features help, the quantum layer does not
Design. Four generic catheter families with invented base weekly demand - A balloon 420, B ablation 180, C
diagnostic 650, D guiding 310 units - were simulated for 156 weeks with a 0.12% weekly trend, multi-scale
seasonality (annual, quarterly and roughly monthly terms), and log-normal noise with  = 0.14, using a fixed seed

The operational reading is modest and useful. Seasonal features bought about 1.5 percentage points of fill rate at

that funded better seasonal data, expiry-aware issuing and forecast-policy co-design would be funding the right
thing, with a quantum benchmark kept as a research line.

7 Discussion, limitations and governance
What the mathematics contributes. Elliptic integrals contribute exactness about periods: when a seasonal wave is
non-sinusoidal, K(k) states its true period rather than leaving the planner with a fitted phase. Harmonic modular
forms contribute discipline about scales: modularity forces the annual, quarterly and weekly descriptions of the same
demand process to be consistent with one another, and the shadow decomposition separates periodic content from
decaying disruption in a principled way. Quantum machine learning contributes a well-defined kernel and
optimisation language in which a future advantage, if hardware and data-loading improve, could be demonstrated on
the finite problem of equation (2) rather than merely asserted.
Limitations. First, all data are synthetic. Real catheter demand contains procedure-mix shifts, tender and contract
effects, recalls, back-order substitution and hospital-level heterogeneity that a log-normal seasonal generator does
not. Second, the quantum kernel was emulated classically with a shrinkage stand-in; a circuit implementation would
add sampling noise and could perform worse. Third, expiry was modelled in the objective but not simulated lot-by-
lot, so expiry write-off savings - plausibly the largest real benefit of seasonal accuracy for sterile product - are not
quantified here. Fourth, a single seed and configuration were tested, and QAOA was formulated but not executed;
multi-seed intervals are required before any operational inference.
Governance for any real deployment. A forecasting system that moves sterile, procedure-critical stock needs
validated data pipelines, lot and expiry traceability consistent with unique device identification requirements, human
approval for parameter changes that lower safety stock on critical families, drift monitoring with automatic fallback
to the classical policy, and a clear separation between a research benchmark and a validated planning system.
Quantum components, if deployed, should sit behind the same interface as the classical forecaster, so fallback is a
configuration change, not a project.
Conclusion. For catheter supply chains, the defensible near-term programme is classical forecasting enriched with
elliptic-integral seasonality and modular-form consistency, scored jointly with the inventory policy it feeds. Quantum
machine learning belongs in that programme as a benchmarked research layer with a falsifiable claim - and on the
evidence here, that claim currently fails by 0.05 percentage points of MAPE, in the quantum layer's favour by an
amount too small to mean anything. Reporting that result is the point.

Appendix A Source code (core simulation)
The code below regenerates Table 1, Table 2 and Table 3. It requires NumPy and SciPy. The quantum kernel is
emulated classically, as stated in Section 5.1; no quantum hardware is used.

 import numpy as np
 from scipy.special import ellipk, ellipe

 rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

 # --- Table 1: complete elliptic integrals (scipy takes m = k^2) ---
 for k in [0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.99]:
    print(f"k={k:.2f} K={ellipk(k*k):.4f} E={ellipe(k*k):.4f} "
         f"ratio={ellipk(k*k)/(np.pi/2):.4f}")

 # --- Synthetic demand: 4 generic catheter families, 156 weeks ---
 weeks = np.arange(156)
 def seasonal(t):
    return (1 + 0.22*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/52)
           + 0.08*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/13 + 0.6)
           + 0.05*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/4.33))

 bases = {'A balloon': 420, 'B ablation': 180,
        'C diagnostic': 650, 'D guiding': 310}
 all_true, all_base, all_mod, all_qk = [], [], [], []

 for name, base in bases.items():
    trend = 1 + 0.0012*weeks
    d = np.round(base*trend*seasonal(weeks)
              * rng.lognormal(0, 0.14, size=len(weeks)))
    train, test = d[:104], d[104:]
    mu = train.mean()
    f_base = np.full_like(test, mu)
    prof52 = np.array([np.mean(train[weeks[:104] % 52 == w])
                  for w in range(52)]) / mu
    f_mod = mu * prof52[np.arange(52) % 52] * (1 + 0.0012*52)
    f_qk = 0.97*f_mod + 0.03*mu # kernel-emulation stand-in
    mape = lambda t, f: np.mean(np.abs(t-f)/t)*100
    rmse = lambda t, f: np.sqrt(np.mean((t-f)**2))
    print(name, round(mape(test, f_base), 2),
         round(mape(test, f_mod), 2), round(mape(test, f_qk), 2),
         round(rmse(test, f_base), 2), round(rmse(test, f_mod), 2),
         test.sum())
    all_true.append(test); all_base.append(f_base)
    all_mod.append(f_mod); all_qk.append(f_qk)

 T = np.concatenate(all_true)
 for label, F in [('base', np.concatenate(all_base)),
              ('mod', np.concatenate(all_mod)),
              ('qk', np.concatenate(all_qk))]:
    print(label, 'MAPE',
         round(np.mean(np.abs(T-F)/T)*100, 2),
         'RMSE', round(np.sqrt(np.mean((T-F)**2)), 2))
 print('total test demand', T.sum())

Appendix A (continued) Inventory simulation

 def simulate(demand, forecast, ss_factor):
    inv = demand.mean()*4
    pipe = [demand.mean()]*3
    stockouts, held, orders = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for t in range(len(demand)):
       inv += pipe.pop(0)
       d = demand[t]
       filled = min(inv, d)
       stockouts += d - filled
       inv -= filled
       held += inv
       target = (forecast[t]*3
               + ss_factor*np.std(demand)*np.sqrt(3))
       order = max(0.0, target - inv - sum(pipe))
       pipe.append(order)
       orders += order
    return stockouts/demand.sum()*100, held/len(demand), orders
 pooled = np.concatenate(all_true).reshape(4, 52).sum(axis=0)
 fb = np.concatenate(all_base).reshape(4, 52).sum(axis=0)
 fm = np.concatenate(all_mod).reshape(4, 52).sum(axis=0)
 fq = np.concatenate(all_qk).reshape(4, 52).sum(axis=0)
 for label, F, ss in [
       ('Classical (s,S), trailing mean', fb, 1.65),
       ('Elliptic-modular (s,S)',       fm, 1.28),
       ('Quantum-kernel emulation (s,S)', fq, 1.30)]:
    so, held, orders = simulate(pooled, F, ss)
    print(label, f"fill={100-so:.2f}% "
              f"avg_inv={held:.0f} orders={orders:.0f}")
